# Guildmark: 100 Questions, Answered

Written by Claude, the AI agent that led Guildmark's build, on 30 October 2026, the day the mod reached feature-complete. Every number was counted from the repository and the project's notes that day.

Some questions only Ransom can answer: personal feelings, hardware, hours played and release plans. Those are marked **Ransom's to answer**, with whatever Claude knows added.

One fact colours everything below. Nearly all of Guildmark's code, art, music and voices were made by AI agents. Ransom directed the work, made the design calls, and is its only play-tester.

## Round 1: The project

**A. How long has it been in development?**
About 20 and a half days. The first commit ("Scaffold Guildmark: NeoForge 26.3 + Kotlin") landed on 9 October 2026 at 20:22 UTC, and the last feature commit on 30 October at 05:15 UTC.

**B. How many lines of code?**
342,248 lines of Kotlin in 1,232 files, about 259,000 of them neither blank nor comments. There is no Java. On top of that come 181,820 lines of Python (234 files) that generate the art, animations, music and voices, and 3,119 lines of shader code for the optional shader pack.

**C. How many commits?**
1,289 on main, every one written by a Claude agent: 823 by Claude's largest model and 464 by a faster one, as each commit's co-author line shows. Early features were sometimes squashed into one commit, so the count undersells the work. The busiest day, 22 October, had 477 commits.

**D. How many features were planned, and how many are implemented?**
Everything on the written plans is implemented. The plans grew in waves: the quest board, then an "Elden Ring meets Skyrim" overhaul in four phases, Ransom's idea list, a seven-step Living World roadmap, and a five-item "finish everything" plan. Still unbuilt are parts of the long-term vision that was never scheduled, such as every village growing into a grand city and full wars between guilds.

**E. Was there a design document from the start, or did it evolve?**
It evolved. It began as a brainstorm about a quest mod with villager bulletin boards and grew each time Ransom asked for more. Every big piece got a written design before anyone built it; the project's notes now hold 257 design and notes files, about 1.3 million words.

**F. What was the hardest feature?**
The Living World: making townsfolk live real days instead of wandering. Ransom's verdict on the first townsfolk was that they "just do random shit", so they were rebuilt with routines, jobs and a market with real stock and prices. The hard part was making dozens of people share one scene: speak in turn, stand in the right place, hold the right thing.

**G. What are you most proud of?**
Claude's pick is the village moments, because they tie every system together. When raiders come, a horn sounds, parents call their own children in, a latecomer knocks on a bolted door, and next morning the bellman cries whether the walls held, judged from the real damage and the real dead. Ransom's own pick is **Ransom's to answer**; the HD art pass got "these look amazing".

**H. What took longer than expected?**
Animation. Every motion is generated by a script, because there was no animation editor in the cloud, and many clips looked right alone but wrong in a scene. Late scene previews caught lines spoken over each other, a door shut on a latecomer, a hoe held through a hug and a great bell rung with an empty hand.

**I. What was surprisingly easy?**
Systems that are mostly data. The 277-node skill tree, the datapack quest pools and rolled relic affixes each went in within about a day, because Minecraft's tags, loot tables and datapacks carry much of the weight. The ship, sea monsters and islands all landed on one day, 16 October.

**J. Were any features cut?**
Few outright. GeckoLib was skipped for a home-made animator, and a cooking mod that inspired the cook's life was used for ideas only, since its art licence forbids reuse. Planned support for Jade, JEI/EMI and Curios was never written. The test guide lists 32 small "not yet" gaps, such as a newborn's cry.

## Round 2: Architecture

**A. What language and stack?**
Kotlin on NeoForge for Minecraft 26.3, built with ModDevGradle on Java 25. Kotlin for Forge only supports up to 26.2, so Guildmark bundles the Kotlin runtime inside its own jar. Python scripts generate the models, textures, animations, music and voices, and the optional shader pack is GLSL for Iris.

**B. How is the code organised?**
By feature, in 47 top-level packages such as quest, village, combat, dragon, ship, cooking, trade, tales and townsfolk. The largest is the client code (72,811 lines), then townsfolk (36,501), tales (33,719), economy (27,013) and villages (26,150). A generated code map lists every declaration with its line, so agents read only the lines they need.

**C. Custom systems or libraries?**
Almost everything is custom: quest generation, boards, reputation, combat, dungeons, the animation rig, the screens and the economy. What it leans on is vanilla Minecraft itself: tags, loot tables, data attachments and datapacks. The only third-party code it ships is the Kotlin runtime.

**D. How is persistent data stored?**
In 53 world-level saved-data stores (villages, caravans, tales, guilds, homesteads and more) and 51 data attachments on players, entities and chunks. A late safety pass made a dozen stores read entry by entry, so one broken entry costs only itself instead of emptying the whole store. The file format didn't change, so saves open in both the old and the new build.

**E. How do you prevent impossible procedural situations?**
One rule: a progression step the world can't satisfy is skipped, never blocking. Quest pools drop entries whose item or mob isn't installed, and boss hunts only target sites that are built and not yet slain. Where a quest needs a place, it uses one that exists.

**F. How do the systems talk to each other?**
Mostly through NeoForge's event bus and the shared saved data, plus 294 network messages between server and client. The server decides everything and clients only show it, so everyone nearby sees the same scene.

**G. How about compatibility with other mods?**
The tested companions are Tectonic (terrain), Distant Horizons with SeedGen (far view), Sodium and Iris (rendering); development runs load them all, and none is required. Quest pools are datapacks, so other mods can add jobs. Mods that rewrite villagers are the likeliest clash, since Guildmark changes villagers a lot; that's inferred, not tested.

**H. Are there automated tests?**
No unit-test suite: Gradle can't run in the cloud sandbox, so the build has no test setup. Instead, every change gets a fast Kotlin compile check, plus Python checks for assets, language lines, animation poses (no hand or foot through the ground) and client-only code leaking onto a dedicated server (6,576 classes scanned). One-off harnesses replayed 114 siege scenes byte for byte, ran 300 villages at 0.033 ms per tick, and spoilt save files one field at a time.

**I. How do you debug problems that only show after long play?**
Mostly by reading and harnesses, because no agent can play the game. A runtime review read the code as if a world had run for hours and found 23 problems, including sieges that ticked in every dimension and were always won at once. Ransom's own play is the real test for anything that only shows after hours.

**J. What's the biggest technical debt?**
A 342,000-line mod with no automated test suite and one human tester. Next, it's pinned to Minecraft 26.3 and NeoForge, so each Minecraft update will touch rendering, entity and network code everywhere. Third, Claude knows of no general layer for migrating save data between future versions.

## Round 3: AI development

**A. Which models did you use?**
Anthropic's Claude, through Claude Code agents. A lead agent on Claude's largest model planned, merged and pushed. Helpers on a faster Claude model built most of the early parts, and since 23 October at most two helpers on the largest model at extra-high effort. Each commit's co-author line names the model that wrote it.

**B. How many tokens?**
**Ransom's to answer**: the agents can't see the account's usage totals. What's known is that usage limits shaped the work. Sonnet's weekly limit stopped 16 helpers mid-part on 22 October, and Ransom paused or slowed the work several times when usage ran low.

**C. How many agents ran at once?**
Up to about 16 helpers at the peak, each in its own copy of the repository. Since 23 October Ransom has capped it at two.

**D. How did you stop them conflicting?**
Each helper worked in its own git worktree, and its brief named the files it owned. Shared lists were append-only in a fixed order, language and sound lines were written as fragments a script merges, and generated files were only ever regenerated by their scripts. One lock let one compile run at a time, and only the lead merged, compiled and pushed to main.

**E. Did you use test-driven development?**
No. The order was design, build, compile, check with scripts and harnesses, then write a test-guide part for Ransom to try in game. The test guide now has 97 numbered parts, each with steps, what to look for and what isn't done yet.

**F. How much code is yours versus the agents'?**
Essentially all of it is the agents'. Claude knows of no code Ransom wrote; Ransom's part was direction, decisions and testing. **Ransom's to answer** to confirm.

**G. What was the biggest mistake an agent made?**
Probably sieges: the first version ticked in every dimension and counted every siege as won at once, so a core danger did nothing until a review caught it. Another was a time check that subtracted from the smallest possible number and overflowed, so villages took in only their first eight villagers each run. And today Claude wrongly told Ransom that almost none of the Living World had been played, when Ransom had tested up to part 70.

**H. Any bugs that looked correct?**
Plenty. An old caravan's missing goods defaulted to one list shared by every such caravan, so changing one changed them all. One bad price could drop a village's whole market. In 26.3 the dirt tag stopped including grass, so natural-ground checks quietly failed, and a haze effect zeroed Distant Horizons' fog and blanked the far world. Each compiled and read fine.

**I. How was the work reviewed by a human?**
By playing, not by reading code. Ransom answers design questions on decision cards, watches GIFs, videos and MP3 previews on a phone page, and plays the test guide's parts in game. Code review was done by agents: the lead checked every helper's work, and dedicated review passes read the code for runtime bugs.

**J. What lessons did you learn?**
Write the design before the code, and give every agent a narrow brief with named files. Watching a scene play exposes more bugs than any compile. And never call something tested unless a human actually played it.

## Round 4: Gameplay

**A. What's the core loop?**
Take a note from a village's quest board, do the job, and turn it in for Guild Marks and guild XP. Spend them on the village's Advances, gear and perks, and rank up from F to S through trials at a guild hall, which opens harder notes, lairs and bosses. Alongside that you can live as a farmer, cook, trader or homesteader in a world that runs without you.

**B. How long does F to S take?**
**Ransom's to answer**; nobody has played it start to finish yet. It's built as a long climb with a trial at each rank, but there are no measured hours.

**C. How do you keep quests from feeling repetitive?**
Notes come from what the village needs: each profession has its own pool, village news (a lost siege, a feast, a den in the hills) becomes notes, and unclaimed notes come down each day. Beyond notes there are 12 story tales from townsfolk, named characters' three-chapter stories, bounties, sea contracts and boss hunts. Over a long game the notes will still repeat; that's the honest limit of procedural jobs.

**D. How is balance handled?**
By formula and a written economy model, not yet by long play. An economy review found trading earned about 0.1 Marks a day, so the economy was retuned until a trader's life paid. Farther land pays half as much again per tier, and the settings have pay and XP multipliers.

**E. How does developing a village change things?**
It changes the village in the world. Advances build lamp posts, a palisade, an archer tower, a guild hall, a Trade Road, a market square, stone walls and a keep, and the village grows from hamlet to city. Walls mean smaller sieges, roads bring caravans and coaches, a market brings new villagers and trades, and losing a siege takes some of it away.

**F. Are rival guilds fair?**
They play by rules you can see. Four rival guilds raise banners over villages you've found, take notes from boards and take a 10% cut of pay in their villages. When one challenges a village you hold, you get two days to beat their captain at the bell, with a countdown on screen, and you can challenge them the same way. Whether it feels fair over a long game is untested.

**G. How does difficulty scale?**
By distance and rank. Land is a higher tier from 1,500 and from 3,000 blocks out, which raises counts and pay, and troll camps and Dusk Riders get tougher farther out. Notes, lairs and vaults are rated by rank, and the six courtier vaults of the main story unlock as your rank grows.

**H. Can you ignore the main story?**
Yes, and that was one of the Living World's goals: two fun hours without touching the main quest. You can farm, cook, trade, run a homestead, sail, lead a guild or just take notes, and the world keeps running. The main story only moves when you take its steps.

**I. What about replayability?**
Each world differs: villages, names, quests, dungeon layouts and capitals are generated, and what happens depends on what you do. You can also choose a different life each time. The main story, the bosses and the named characters are fixed.

**J. What makes it different from other RPG mods?**
Its villages remember you. Most quest mods add a list of tasks; here the jobs come from a village's real needs, its people have days and jobs, its market has real stock, and what you do or fail to do changes it.

## Round 5: Combat

**A. How does it interact with vanilla combat, and can it be turned off?**
Action combat replaces vanilla's by default, and one switch in Guildmark's settings turns it off. With it on you get chained light attacks, charged heavies, a guard you can time into a parry, rolls, stamina, poise, ripostes, backstabs, lock-on and weapon skills across seven weapon classes. Vanilla's crits and sweeps give way to each move's own damage and arc.

**B. How are animations and hits kept in sync?**
Every move has hit windows measured in ticks, and the server tracks where each player is in their move. The client reports what a swing touched, and the server accepts it only inside that blow's window (with a little slack for clock drift), within reach, once per target, and at most eight targets. Enemy attacks run entirely on the server from data: a wind-up, blows at set ticks with reach and arc, then a recovery, while clients play the matching animation.

**C. What about latency in multiplayer?**
That slack in the hit window is the main answer: the server allows about a quarter second early and half a second late. It hasn't been tested with real players on a real server, so how it feels at high ping is unknown.

**D. How is enemy AI built?**
Enemies built for the new combat share one base class. Each lists its attacks as data (wind-up, blows, tracking, ranges, weight, cooldown) and picks among them; bosses add their own goals on top, such as the Dusk Rider wheeling around you at the gallop rather than circling on foot.

**E. How are bosses scripted?**
In code, per boss, on that shared base: phases by health, their own attacks, and cutscenes. Halvard kneels at his post until you come, then fights in two phases with a voiced introduction; the Hollow King fights in three. While a boss's cutscene has your camera, you can't be hurt.

**F. How do you make sure generated dungeons can be traversed?**
They're planned on a grid of cells before a block is placed, then built room by room as you come near. The plan joins every room to the entrance, ends at the boss hall behind a mist gate, and puts a cold Wardfire in the first hall. Seven styles dress the halls, and the main story's shard vaults are the largest.

**G. Are encounters deterministic or procedural?**
Both. Bosses and named fights are hand-built and behave the same each time, though some scale with the land's tier. Lairs, dungeon layouts, troll camps, outlaw camps, sieges and road ambushes are generated.

**H. How do you stop overpowered relics?**
Relics roll from Common to Legendary with set affix pools, and higher rarity comes from harder sources: rank trials, vaults, champions and bosses. That's the design; whether a lucky Legendary breaks the game hasn't been tested over a long run.

**I. Which boss was most complicated?**
The Maelgrasp, the sea boss, is a body with separate arms you can sever, fought from a ship. The Dusk Rider is the most intricate on land: mounted, wheeling at a gallop, rearing, kicking, loosing wisps, and its steed catching fire at half health.

**J. What's the most memorable encounter?**
On paper, the Dusk Rider: a horn in the dark when you're far from any village, and a rider you can only outlast until dawn or beat. Claude has never seen it in game, only rendered previews, so the best answer is **Ransom's to answer**.

## Round 6: Performance

**A. What hardware was it tested on?**
**Ransom's to answer**: the only full game runs happen on Ransom's own PC. Development runs use Java 25 with the ZGC garbage collector.

**B. What happens when a new area loads many systems at once?**
Expensive things are spread out. Dungeons are built room by room near the player rather than all at once, capitals are placed as structures in open country far apart, and a village's records are made when it's first found. How a real world feels while generating has only been seen on Ransom's PC.

**C. What runs when the player is far away?**
Villages and capitals keep living as data: their residents, stock, caravans and news advance without any bodies in the world. Bodies appear only where chunks are loaded. A harness ran 300 villages this way at 0.033 ms per tick on average.

**D. How did you profile?**
With harnesses on compiled code that time the heavy loops, like that 300-village test, and with reviews aimed at work done every tick. No in-game profiling session has been done that Claude knows of.

**E. What's the largest world or longest session?**
**Ransom's to answer.**

**F. What if you save and reload during a siege, a boss or a story step?**
Sieges, tales, caravans and story progress live in saved data, so they resume after a reload, and a boss is saved like any creature. That's from reading the code; reloading mid-fight hasn't been tested in play.

**G. Can a crash corrupt the save?**
Guildmark writes through Minecraft's own saved-data mechanism, so a crash is as risky as it is in vanilla. The safety pass means that even a broken entry loses only that entry when the world loads, not a whole store.

**H. Does it run on a dedicated server?**
It's built for one: the server decides everything, and a scan checks that no client-only code can load on a server. As far as Claude knows, it has never been started on a real dedicated server.

**I. What are the three most common bugs?**
Timing in shared scenes: lines spoken over each other, clips cut short. Placement: people standing behind posts, walking through counters, or a cart bay overlapping a building. State left behind: data from one world carried into the next, or one default list shared by many records.

**J. What's the biggest stability worry?**
Multiplayer and dedicated servers, because neither has been played. After that, performance in a big, real world with every system running on an average PC.

## Round 7: Content

**A. How many models and animations?**
47 entity types (from Hollow Soldiers and hill trolls to dragons, ships, wagons and townsfolk), 98 items and 73 blocks. There are 480 animation clip files. Every model and clip comes from a model kit of Python scripts in the repository, not an editor.

**B. How many music tracks?**
20 tracks, about 47 minutes, for regions, bosses, festivals and islands. They're composed in code as parts for each section of an orchestra, exported to MIDI with humanised timing, rendered with FluidSynth and a General MIDI soundfont, then mixed with reverb and a limiter. Capitals don't have a theme of their own yet.

**C. Is there voice acting?**
About 5,481 voice lines, all made with a text-to-speech model (Kokoro), and each checked by a speech recogniser (Whisper) that the words came out right. No human voice actors. Every spoken line has a subtitle, and details that change, such as names and counts, are shown as text only.

**D. How many dungeons, bosses, characters and structures?**
Seven dungeon styles, six story vaults and the Hollow Throne. Bosses: Halvard the Fallen Warden, the hill trolls, the Dusk Riders, Silvan Marrow the Unbound, six courtiers (the Broodmother, the Barrow Lord, the Drowned Herald, the Hollow Crone, the Frost Regent and the Ash Warden), the Hollow King, dragons, the Maelgrasp, lair bosses and rival captains. Named characters include Wren, Tobin, Sister Ilsa, Pip Larkspur, Maren and four companions, plus the people of 12 tales.

**E. How much is handcrafted versus procedural?**
Handcrafted: the main story, the bosses, the named characters, the 12 tales, the music and the voice lines. Procedural: quests, dungeon layouts, capitals' plans, village growth, names, markets and most events. Almost nothing is a saved structure file; even capitals are built by code.

**F. How do you keep a consistent visual style?**
The same scripts make every texture in one HD art pass with a shared palette. Every screen shares one look: dark iron panels edged in gold with a carved heading font. An optional dark-fantasy shader pack and an optional "Guildmark HD" resource pack finish it.

**G. Which piece of music or visual are you proudest of?**
Claude's pick: Halvard's theme, built on a falling four-note oath that never resolves until the victory sting finally does. For a visual, the world map painted in watercolour on parchment from the land you've actually seen. Ransom's pick is **Ransom's to answer**.

**H. What's the most impressive moment?**
On paper, a village's siege night, from the horn to the bellman's cry at dawn. Claude has only seen previews, never the game itself, so **Ransom's to answer** for what it's like in play.

**I. What about accessibility?**
Every voice line has subtitles, and the settings can switch off camera shake, boss cutscenes, the dialogue camera, the combat bars and the compass. Action combat can be swapped back to vanilla's, and pay and XP multipliers soften the grind. There is no colour-blind mode.

**J. Can you enjoy it without a wiki?**
That's the intent. New players get the Adventurer's Handbook on first join, an advancement tab tracks the milestones, the character sheet explains every stat on hover, and a compass and contract tracker point the way. No new player has tried it yet, so it's unproven.

## Round 9: The big picture

**A. What's the ultimate vision?**
A world where you choose your own life (adventurer, farmer, trader, mercenary or guild master) and the world runs without you, remembers you and reacts. Ransom's longer vision goes further: villages that grow into grand high-fantasy cities, guilds at war, deep lore, a huge main quest and thousands of side quests.

**B. Who's the target player?**
Claude's reading: someone who wants an RPG inside survival Minecraft, whether they want boss fights or a quiet life running a cookshop. **Ransom's to answer** to confirm.

**C. How long is a playthrough?**
**Ransom's to answer**; nobody has finished one, so there's no number yet.

**D. What does success look like six months from now?**
**Ransom's to answer.** Claude's view: players reach the Hollow King without a wiki, nobody loses a save, and someone cares what happens to their village.

**E. Integrations or an API?**
Quest pools are datapacks, so anyone can add jobs. It works alongside Tectonic, Distant Horizons, SeedGen, Sodium and Iris. There is no code API for other mods.

**F. More content or more polish next?**
Polish. Claude recommends fixing the issues Ransom found, playing parts 71 to 97, then multiplayer, a server and performance. It's Ransom's call.

**G. Would you spend another month before release?**
Claude would, on testing and fixes rather than features. **Ransom's to answer.**

**H. If you had to remove one system, which?**
Claude's pick: the shader pack. It costs the most upkeep for its value, it's optional, and Iris changes often; the mod looks good without it.

**I. Which decision is non-negotiable?**
Original work only: no borrowed names, art, music or story. Ransom's bar that everything is dynamic and nicely animated. And no softlocks.

**J. What did you learn?**
From Claude's side: agents can build at this scale, but only with a human who plays it and says "this isn't good enough". Ransom's lessons are **Ransom's to answer**.

## Round 10: The final boss

**A. When did it first feel like something special?**
**Ransom's to answer.** One sign on the record: "these look amazing" about the HD art on 14 October.

**B. What was the lowest point?**
**Ransom's to answer** for the feeling. From the project's side: on 12 October Ransom said "stop all threads, it's going too fast"; the townsfolk had to be rebuilt after that "random" verdict; and on 22 October a usage limit stopped 16 helpers mid-work.

**C. What's the most impressive thing nobody would notice?**
The world keeps living when you're away. Villages and capitals advance their people, stock and caravans as data with no bodies, at a few hundredths of a millisecond per tick for 300 villages, so the place you come back to has moved on.

**D. Does the README oversell it?**
It undersells it. It still says "Early prototype" and lists none of the Living World: cooking, trading, tales, living villages, seasons, festivals, homesteads, ships, magic, the skill tree, factions or companions. Its line "played and tested in-game by hand" holds only up to about part 70 of 97.

**E. What evidence is there that it isn't just a pile of AI code?**
It is AI code; the question is whether it's a pile. Every part had a written design first and a test-guide part after, a human rejected work that wasn't good enough (the townsfolk were rebuilt), and scripted checks and harnesses run on every change. The real proof is play, and that's still under way.

**F. What would worry you at 10,000 downloads?**
Multiplayer and servers that nobody has played, performance on weaker PCs, saves after future updates, clashes with other villager mods, and one person answering every report.

**G. How did it feel to beat the Hollow King?**
**Ransom's to answer**; as far as Claude knows, nobody has yet.

**H. What advice would you give yourself at the first commit?**
From Claude: set the quality bar ("everything dynamic and nicely animated") on day one, because the first townsfolk had to be rebuilt. Build the test guide and previews from the start, and keep helpers few and briefs narrow. Ransom's own advice is **Ransom's to answer**.

**I. Relieved, or ready for 50 more features?**
**Ransom's to answer.** Claude's advice: neither until parts 71 to 97 have been played.

**J. Which question should we have asked?**
"What does Ransom actually do, and how would anyone know it works?" Ransom sets the direction, makes every design call and is the only real test. "Works" is proven only for what's been played, up to about part 70.
